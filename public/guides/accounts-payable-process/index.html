What Is Accounts Payable? The AP Process, Step by Step

By Vettlo Editorial TeamUpdated Nov 10, 2026 · Prices verified Nov 10, 2026

Short answer

Accounts payable (AP) is the money a business owes its suppliers for goods and services it has received but not yet paid for, recorded as a current liability on the balance sheet. The AP process turns each vendor invoice into a payment in six steps: set up the vendor, capture the invoice, match it to the order and delivery, approve it, pay it by the due date, and record and reconcile the payment.

What is accounts payable?

Accounts payable is the total of unpaid bills from vendors: the office lease invoice, the supplier who shipped inventory on net 30 terms, the contractor who sent an invoice last week. Each bill is a short-term debt, so AP sits under current liabilities on the balance sheet, and it shrinks as you pay. In most small businesses AP lives in the bills or purchases module of the accounting software; our comparison of the best accounting software ranks 6 tools, and this guide covers the process itself.

Three terms get mixed up with AP:

How is accounts payable recorded?

Under accrual accounting, a bill hits AP when you receive it, not when you pay it. That's two journal entries per bill. Using a $1,200 supplier invoice as an example:

EventDebitCreditEffect on AP
Bill received and enteredExpense or inventory $1,200Accounts payable $1,200Up $1,200
Bill paidAccounts payable $1,200Cash $1,200Down $1,200

So accounts payable is a credit balance: entering a bill credits it, paying a bill debits it. Accounting software makes both entries for you when you enter a bill and later mark it paid.

A business keeping its books on the cash method records the expense only when it pays, so there is no AP balance in the ledger. It still needs a list of unpaid bills to know what's due, which is why most tools keep a bills list either way.

What are the steps in the accounts payable process?

The AP process has six steps, from adding a vendor to closing out the payment. Small teams often do all six with one or two people; larger ones split them so no single person can both add a vendor and pay it.

  1. Set up the vendor. Record the legal name, address, payment details and terms, and collect a Form W-9 from contractors and other vendors you may need to report, so you have their taxpayer ID. Under the IRS instructions for tax years beginning after 2025, you report nonemployee compensation of $2,000 or more on Form 1099-NEC (up from $600), and payments to corporations generally aren't reported, with exceptions such as attorneys' fees. Forms are due January 31.
  2. Capture the invoice. Get every bill into one place: a dedicated AP email inbox, uploads, or receipt scanning in the accounting tool. Enter the vendor, invoice number, date, due date, amount and the account or item it belongs to.
  3. Match and verify. Check the invoice against what you ordered and what arrived (see three-way matching below), and check the invoice number against bills already entered to catch duplicates.
  4. Approve. The person who ordered or owns the budget confirms the bill is valid. Set a rule for who approves what, for example any bill above a set amount needs a second approver.
  5. Schedule and pay. Pay by the due date, or by the discount date if the vendor offers one, by ACH, check or card. Batching payments once or twice a week keeps it manageable.
  6. Record and reconcile. Mark the bill paid so AP drops, match the payment to the bank statement, and keep the invoice with proof of payment. At month end, the unpaid bills report should agree with the AP balance on the balance sheet.

This summarizes IRS reporting rules and isn't tax advice; check the current IRS instructions or ask your accountant about your vendors.

What is three-way matching?

Three-way matching means paying an invoice only when it agrees with two other documents: the purchase order (what you agreed to buy) and the receiving record (what actually arrived). It stops you paying for goods that were never delivered, or at a price you never agreed.

DocumentWho creates itWhat you compare
Purchase orderYour business, when orderingItem, quantity, agreed unit price, terms
Receiving record (packing slip or goods received note)Whoever accepts the deliveryQuantity actually received, condition
Vendor invoiceThe supplierQuantity billed, price, total, due date

Two-way matching compares only the purchase order and the invoice. It suits services and subscriptions, where there's no delivery to count. Many small businesses don't issue purchase orders at all; for them the practical version is checking each bill against the contract or quote and confirming with whoever ordered it that the work was done.

When the documents don't agree, don't pay the difference: hold the bill, ask the vendor for a corrected invoice or a credit note, and pay the amount you can match.

How do net 30 and 2/10 net 30 payment terms work?

Payment terms say how many days you have to pay after the invoice date. Net 30 means the full amount is due in 30 days. 2/10 net 30 means you can take 2% off if you pay within 10 days; otherwise the full amount is due in 30.

TermMeaning
Due on receiptPay as soon as you get the invoice
Net 15 / net 30 / net 60Full amount due 15, 30 or 60 days after the invoice date
2/10 net 302% discount if paid within 10 days, full amount due in 30
EOMCounted from the end of the month the invoice was issued

Early-payment discounts are worth more than they look. On a $1,200 bill, 2/10 net 30 saves $24 for paying 20 days early. Skipping it is like borrowing $1,176 for 20 days at 2/98, which works out to about 37% a year (2/98 × 365/20). If you have the cash, taking the discount usually beats holding it.

Without a discount, paying on the due date rather than early keeps cash in the business longer. Finance teams track this as days payable outstanding (DPO): average accounts payable ÷ cost of goods sold × the days in the period. A higher DPO means you take longer to pay; a sudden rise can also mean bills are getting stuck in approval.

How do you prevent duplicate payments and invoice fraud?

Two things go wrong in AP: paying the same bill twice, and paying a fake or altered one. These controls address both:

  • Separate duties. The person who adds or edits a vendor shouldn't also approve and release its payments. In a two-person business, have the owner approve payments the bookkeeper prepares.
  • Verify bank detail changes. When a vendor asks to change where you send money, confirm by calling a phone number you already have on file, not one in the request.
  • Block duplicates. Enter invoice numbers exactly and let the software warn on a repeat vendor and number.
  • Pay only real vendors. The FTC's May 2026 alert to small businesses advises checking all invoices closely, having clear procedures for approving purchases and invoices from vendors you actually work with, and knowing who you're dealing with.
  • Keep the paper trail. The IRS says supporting documents should identify the payee, the amount paid, proof of payment, the date incurred and a description showing it was a business expense; invoices and canceled checks or bank transfer records together do that. Keep them at least until the period of limitations for the return runs out, generally 3 years from the date you filed.

FAQ

Is accounts payable an asset or a liability?

Accounts payable is a liability. It's money the business owes vendors, usually within weeks, so it's listed under current liabilities on the balance sheet.

Is accounts payable a debit or a credit?

Accounts payable normally carries a credit balance. Entering a vendor bill credits AP and debits an expense or inventory account; paying the bill debits AP and credits cash.

What is the difference between accounts payable and accounts receivable?

Accounts payable is what you owe suppliers for goods and services you received. Accounts receivable is what customers owe you for invoices you sent. AP is a liability; AR is an asset.

Do I need to send a 1099 to every vendor?

No. For tax years beginning after 2025, the IRS instructions require Form 1099-NEC for nonemployee compensation of $2,000 or more, and payments to corporations generally aren't reported, with exceptions such as attorneys' fees. Collect a W-9 when you set up the vendor so you can tell. This isn't tax advice.
