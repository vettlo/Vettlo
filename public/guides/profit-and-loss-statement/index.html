Profit and Loss Statement: How to Read and Prepare One

By Vettlo Editorial TeamUpdated Nov 24, 2026 · Prices verified Nov 24, 2026

Short answer

A profit and loss statement (P&L, also called an income statement) shows a business's revenue, costs and expenses over a period, such as a month, quarter or year, and ends with net income: whether the business made or lost money in that period. It reads top to bottom: revenue, minus cost of goods sold, gives gross profit; minus operating expenses gives operating income; then other income and expenses (and, for businesses that pay it, income tax) give net income.

What is a profit and loss statement?

A profit and loss statement is the report that answers "did we make money?" for a stated stretch of time. It adds up every revenue and expense account in your books for that period and subtracts one from the other. Accountants also call it the income statement or the statement of operations; they're the same report.

Any of the tools in our ranking of the best accounting software builds a P&L automatically from the transactions you record, so the work is less about typing the report and more about making sure the numbers under it are right and knowing how to read it. This guide covers both.

The P&L is one of three core financial statements, and each answers a different question:

StatementQuestion it answersCovers
Profit and loss (income statement)Did the business earn more than it spent?A period: a month, quarter or year
Balance sheetWhat does the business own and owe?A single date
Cash flow statementWhere did cash come from and go?A period

The P&L draws only on revenue and expense accounts; assets, liabilities and equity sit on the balance sheet. Our chart of accounts guide shows which accounts feed which statement.

What goes on a profit and loss statement, line by line?

A standard small business P&L has six building blocks in a fixed order, with a subtotal after each group of costs.

LineWhat it includesHow it's calculated
Revenue (sales, income)Sales of products and services, less returns, refunds and discountsGross sales − returns and discounts = net revenue
Cost of goods sold (COGS)Direct costs of what you sold: materials, inventory, production labor, fulfillmentListed by account
Gross profitWhat's left to run the business after direct costsNet revenue − COGS
Operating expensesCosts of running the business that don't vary directly with each sale: wages, rent, advertising, software, depreciationListed by account
Operating incomeProfit from the core businessGross profit − operating expenses
Other income and expenses, taxesInterest earned or paid, one-off gains or losses, and income tax for businesses taxed as corporationsOperating income ± other items − tax = net income

Single-step vs multi-step format

The table above is the multi-step format: it shows gross profit and operating income as separate subtotals. A single-step P&L lists all revenue, then all expenses, and shows only one result, net income. Service businesses with no inventory often have no COGS section at all, so their gross profit equals revenue and the two formats look almost the same. If you sell products, the multi-step layout is the useful one, because gross margin is the first number that tells you whether your pricing works.

Does a sole proprietorship's P&L include income tax?

Usually not. A sole proprietorship doesn't pay income tax as a business: the owner reports the result on Schedule C (Form 1040), "Profit or Loss From Business," and the IRS instructions say to enter the net profit or loss on line 31 and include it on Schedule 1 (Form 1040), line 3. So a sole proprietor's P&L normally ends at net income before tax. Partnerships and S corporations also pass income to their owners; C corporations show income tax expense on the P&L.

The three margins sum it up:

What this P&L says

  • Sales grew, profit didn't. Net revenue rose 14.4%, but net income moved only +0.6%, from $6,850 to $6,890.
  • Gross margin slipped from 58.8% to 57.3%. Had the shop kept its earlier margin, the latest quarter would have produced about $745 more gross profit. Materials and fulfillment grew faster than sales, which points at supplier prices or shipping rates.
  • Advertising absorbed the growth. Ad spend went from $4,200 to $6,900, which is most of the increase in operating expenses. The question for the owner is whether the extra sales came from those ads and will repeat.
  • Returns rose faster than sales: from 3.3% to 4.3% of product sales.

None of that shows up if you look only at the bottom line, which is why the change and percentage columns matter more than the totals.

How do you prepare a profit and loss statement?

To prepare a P&L, get the period's books complete and correct, then let your software total the revenue and expense accounts; if you work in a spreadsheet, you do the same totals by hand from the general ledger.

  1. Pick the period and the accounting basis. Monthly is the usual rhythm, with quarterly and annual roll-ups. Choose cash or accrual and stay consistent; our cash vs accrual accounting guide explains how the choice moves revenue and expenses between months.
  2. Record everything for the period. Every invoice, sales receipt, bill and expense, including card charges and anything paid personally for the business.
  3. Reconcile the bank and card accounts to their statements, so no transaction is missing or doubled. See our bank reconciliation guide.
  4. Post adjusting entries on accrual books: depreciation, prepaid expenses used up, and expenses incurred but not yet billed.
  5. Check the classification. Direct costs belong in COGS, overhead in operating expenses. Uncategorized income or expense accounts with balances are a sign of transactions still waiting to be sorted.
  6. Run the report and compare it with the previous period and the same period last year, then investigate any line that moved more than you expected.

Why a transaction might be missing from your P&L

Some transactions never appear on a P&L by design. Intuit's QuickBooks help lists the common cases: a customer payment on its own touches only accounts receivable and the bank account, and a bill payment touches only accounts payable and the bank account. Revenue appears when you record the invoice or sales receipt, and an expense when you record the bill, check or expense against an expense account. That's how double-entry books work; see our general ledger guide for how entries post.

What doesn't belong on a profit and loss statement?

Anything that changes what the business owns or owes, rather than what it earned or spent, stays off the P&L. Putting these items on it is the most common reason a small business P&L looks wrong.

ItemWhere it goes insteadWhat reaches the P&L
Loan proceeds and principal repaymentsA loan liability on the balance sheetOnly the interest, as interest expense
Equipment, vehicles and other long-lived assetsA fixed asset on the balance sheetDepreciation, spread over the asset's useful life
Owner's draws and owner contributionsEquityNothing
Inventory you bought but haven't soldInventory, an assetCost of goods sold, when the items sell
Sales tax collected from customersA sales tax liability until you pay itNothing; it isn't your revenue
Transfers between your own accountsBoth bank or card accountsNothing

This is also why a profitable P&L and a shrinking bank balance can both be true: loan repayments, equipment purchases, inventory build-up and owner's draws all use cash without touching profit. On accrual books, unpaid customer invoices count as revenue before the cash arrives; our accounts receivable guide covers keeping those collected.

General accounting guidance, not tax or legal advice; your accountant may classify some items differently.

FAQ

Is a profit and loss statement the same as an income statement?

Yes. Profit and loss statement, P&L, income statement and statement of operations are names for the same report: revenue minus costs and expenses over a period, ending in net income or net loss.

What is the difference between gross profit and net income?

Gross profit is revenue minus the direct cost of what you sold (cost of goods sold). Net income is what remains after also subtracting operating expenses, interest, other items and, for businesses that pay it, income tax. Gross profit shows whether your pricing covers direct costs; net income shows whether the whole business made money.

How often should a small business run a P&L?

Monthly is the common rhythm, after the bank and card accounts are reconciled, because a problem caught one month in is cheaper to fix than one found at tax time. Quarterly and year-to-date versions help with estimated taxes and year-over-year comparisons.

Can I make a profit and loss statement in Excel or Google Sheets?

Yes. List your revenue accounts, cost of goods sold and operating expenses with their totals for the period, then add formulas for gross profit, operating income and net income. The totals must come from complete, reconciled records, which is the part accounting software automates.
