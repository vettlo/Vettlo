Double-Entry Bookkeeping: How Debits and Credits Work

By Vettlo Editorial TeamUpdated Nov 17, 2026 · Prices verified Nov 17, 2026

Short answer

Double-entry bookkeeping records every transaction in at least two accounts: one or more debits and one or more credits of equal total, so the books stay in balance. Debits increase asset and expense accounts, credits increase liability, equity and revenue accounts, and after every entry assets still equal liabilities plus equity.

What is double-entry bookkeeping?

Double-entry bookkeeping is the method behind almost every set of business books: each transaction is written as an entry that debits at least one account and credits at least one other for the same total. Buy a $50 printer with cash and two things change at once: you have $50 more equipment and $50 less cash. Double entry records both sides.

It's also the method accounting software runs on, whichever product you choose. Our comparison of the best accounting software ranks 6 tools on price and features; this guide covers the rules those tools apply when they write entries for you.

The idea rests on the accounting equation:

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

Everything the business owns (assets) was paid for either with borrowed money (liabilities) or with the owner's money and retained profit (equity). Because every entry changes two sides by the same amount, the equation can't drift out of balance. IRS Publication 583 calls the system "self-balancing because you record every transaction as a debit entry in one account and as a credit entry in another."

Revenue and expenses sit inside equity: revenue raises it, expenses lower it. Written out, the expanded equation is Assets = Liabilities + Owner's capital + Revenue − Expenses, which is why sales and costs follow the same debit and credit logic as the balance sheet.

How do debits and credits work?

A debit is an entry on the left side of an account and a credit is an entry on the right; whether each one increases or decreases the balance depends on the account type. Assets and expenses go up with debits. Liabilities, equity and revenue go up with credits.

Account typeA debitA creditNormal balanceExamples
AssetIncreasesDecreasesDebitCash, receivables, equipment
ExpenseIncreasesDecreasesDebitRent, supplies, wages
LiabilityDecreasesIncreasesCreditSupplier bills, loans, sales tax owed
EquityDecreasesIncreasesCreditOwner's capital, retained earnings
RevenueDecreasesIncreasesCreditSales, service fees

"Debit" and "credit" here have nothing to do with good or bad, or with your bank card. They only mean left and right. That's why your bank statement calls a deposit a credit: to the bank, your money is a liability it owes you. On your own books, the same deposit is a debit to cash.

A common memory aid is DEAL versus ER: Dividends (or owner's draws), Expenses and Assets increase with a debit; Liabilities, Equity and Revenue increase with a credit. Owner's draws are a contra-equity account, so they run the opposite way from capital.

To write any entry, ask three questions:

  1. Which accounts changed? Every transaction touches at least two.
  2. What type is each account? Look it up on your chart of accounts, which lists every account by type.
  3. Did each one go up or down? Use the table: an asset going up is a debit, a liability going up is a credit, and so on. Then check that total debits equal total credits.

Single-entry vs double-entry bookkeeping: what's the difference?

Single-entry bookkeeping records each transaction once, as income or an expense in a running list; double entry records it in two or more accounts and tracks assets and debts as well. Single entry is simpler, but it can't produce a balance sheet and has no built-in check for errors.

Single entryDouble entry
Records per transactionOne line: income or expenseTwo or more lines: debits and credits of equal total
Based onThe income statement (profit or loss)Journals and ledgers for every account
Tracks assets and liabilitiesNot in the main record; kept in separate lists if at allYes, each has its own account
ReportsIncome, expenses and profitProfit and loss, balance sheet, trial balance
Error checkNone built inDebits must equal credits
Typical fitA new, very small business with few transactionsAny business with receivables, payables, loans, inventory or employees

The IRS lets you choose. Publication 583 says single entry "can be a simple and practical system if you are starting a small business," recording income and expenses through a daily summary of cash receipts and monthly summaries of receipts and disbursements. It adds that double entry "uses journals and ledgers," and that total debits must equal total credits after posting; if they don't, there's an error to find.

A spreadsheet of income and expenses is single entry. You move to double entry, in practice, the day you start using accounting software, because it records both sides automatically.

How does accounting software handle double entry?

Accounting software writes the journal entry for you every time you save a standard form, so you rarely choose debits and credits yourself. What each everyday action records behind the scenes:

You do thisSoftware debitsSoftware credits
Send an invoiceAccounts receivableRevenue (and sales tax payable, if charged)
Record a customer paymentBank or undeposited fundsAccounts receivable
Enter a supplier billThe expense or asset account on the billAccounts payable
Pay a billAccounts payableBank
Categorize a card or bank chargeThe expense account you pickBank or credit card account
Transfer between your own accountsReceiving accountSending account

The one place you write debits and credits directly is a manual journal entry, used for items with no standard form: depreciation, accrued expenses, loan interest splits and year-end corrections from your accountant.

  • QuickBooks: + New > Journal entry. QuickBooks' help page says to enter each account on its own line, with the same amount in the opposite column, keeping total debits and credits equal.
  • Zoho Books: Accountant > Manual Journals > + New Journal. Zoho's help page tells users to "ensure that the amount debited is always equal to the amount credited."

Both help pages make balanced debits and credits a requirement of the entry, so the arithmetic is the easy part. Picking the right account is the part software can't do for you, which is why the categories you choose matter more than the mechanics. What are the most common double-entry mistakes?

Most double-entry errors still balance, because the debit and credit match but one of them lands in the wrong place. These are the ones bookkeepers most often fix at month-end:

MistakeWhat happensFix
Debit and credit swappedCash goes down when it should go up, so the error shows up as twice the amountReverse the entry and record it again the right way round
Equipment recorded as an expenseProfit is understated and the asset is missing from the balance sheetMove it to a fixed-asset account and depreciate it
Owner's draws recorded as wages or expensesProfit is understated; draws aren't a business expenseUse an owner's draw (contra-equity) account
Loan payments booked fully to expenseThe loan balance never goes downSplit each payment: principal debits the loan, interest debits interest expense
Customer payment recorded as new revenueRevenue counted twice and receivables never clearApply the payment to the open invoice so it credits receivables

A monthly bank reconciliation catches many of these, since the books' cash balance won't match the statement; our bank reconciliation guide walks through it.

FAQ

What is an example of double-entry bookkeeping?

Paying $1,100 of rent from the business checking account is recorded as a $1,100 debit to rent expense and a $1,100 credit to cash. The expense goes up, cash goes down, and the debit and credit totals match.

Does a debit always mean an increase?

No. A debit increases asset, expense and owner's draw accounts, and decreases liability, equity and revenue accounts. A credit does the opposite. Whether an entry raises or lowers a balance depends on the account's type.

Is double-entry bookkeeping required for a small business?

Not by the IRS. Publication 583 lets a business choose single-entry or double-entry bookkeeping, as long as the records support the return. Lenders, investors and accountants usually expect double-entry books with a balance sheet, and accounting software keeps them by default.

Can you do double-entry bookkeeping in Excel?

Yes. Use one sheet as a journal with date, account, debit and credit columns, check that each entry's debits and credits match, and summarize balances by account with a pivot table or SUMIF formulas. It works for a few dozen transactions a month; beyond that, bank feeds in accounting software save most of the typing.
